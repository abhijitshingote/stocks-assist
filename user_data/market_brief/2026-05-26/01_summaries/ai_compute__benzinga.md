# AI Compute — benzinga

_kind: theme · elapsed: 148.8s_

AI compute traded with a risk‑on tone across most accelerators and infrastructure names Friday, even as NVIDIA continued to lag after its blockbuster earnings and outsized run.

AI‑linked indices and software remained firm, with **SOXX +2.33%**, **IGV +1.65%**, **XLK +1.00%**, **QQQ +0.42%**, and **SPY +0.39%**, underscoring broad tech strength while capital rotated within the AI stack toward secondary and infrastructure beneficiaries. NVDA diverged from the group, closing **-2.27% at $215.23**, while peers and satellite plays including **AMD +4.98% ($466.49)**, **INTC +1.26% ($119.57)**, **ARM +3.92% ($303.14)**, **ALAB +2.48% ($303.51)**, **CEVA +2.22% ($39.19)**, **AMBA +5.17% ($87.30)**, **SIMO +3.95% ($273.96)**, and **AIP +2.75% ($36.44)** all advanced with the AI‑compute and semiconductor complex.[partial1][partial2][partial3][partial4]

NVIDIA’s weakness came despite exceptional fundamentals and sustained sell‑side conviction. The company reported fiscal Q1 revenue of **$81.6B, +85% Y/Y**, ahead of roughly **$79B** consensus, with **data‑center revenue at $75.2B (~92% of sales)**, non‑GAAP gross margin **75%**, and EPS of **$1.87 vs $1.77** expected, while guiding Q2 revenue to **$91B** and authorizing an **$80B buyback** alongside a dividend hike from **$0.01 to $0.25** per share.[partial3] Benzinga characterized the quarter as a beat where guidance did not blow through the top of expectations, and several pieces framed the subsequent price action as a pullback toward **“key support” near $212** with the stock “slipping 1.2%” intraday and trading **down >5% on the week** as investors digested the print and record valuation around **$5.2–$5.4T**.[partial2][partial3][partial4] Fundamentally, NVDA is described as the **leading AI‑GPU platform** with dominant **CUDA** software and expanding **data‑center networking**, posting **EBITDA of $71.0B**, **gross profit of $61.16B**, and **revenue growth of 85.23%** versus semi‑industry averages, while carrying **P/E 32.98, P/B 26.68, P/S 20.78, ROE 33.06%** and a low **0.06 debt‑to‑equity ratio** that Benzinga interprets as a stronger balance sheet than top peers.[partial1] A separate profile notes NVDA’s **P/E screens lower than the peer group**, with **P/B and P/S higher**, so it appears *cheaper on earnings but richer on book and sales* within high‑growth semis.[partial1] On factor metrics, Benzinga Edge ranks NVDA with **Momentum score ~83rd percentile** and **Growth score 99th percentile**, and one wrap labeled it the “marquee corporate event” driving the S&P 500’s eighth straight weekly gain.[partial1][partial3]

Despite that, positioning and sentiment look more nuanced. Options flow showed **heavy short‑dated trading around the $215–$217.50 strikes**: one scanner flagged a **bearish call trade** at the **$215 strike** expiring May 22 (100 contracts, **$28.2K premium**) alongside large open interest and volume,[partial3] while a separate sweep earlier in the week highlighted a **bullish $217.50 call sweep** expiring May 26 tagged **“CALL – SWEEP – BULLISH,”** with **265 contracts** and **$35.6K** in premium, suggesting active two‑sided speculation rather than one‑way chase.[partial1] On the fundamental side, multiple top‑ranked analysts reiterated positive views after earnings: Wedbush’s Matt Bryson kept **Outperform** and raised his NVDA target from **$300 to $330**, Benchmark’s Cody Acree lifted **$250 to $335**, and Truist’s William Stein raised **$287 to $307**, all following the May 20 beat.[partial1] At the same time, a prediction‑market piece noted that while a Wall Street Journal columnist argued NVDA is still “cheap” given guidance to ~**$91B** in quarterly revenue—nearly **2x** a year earlier—UBS’s Tim Arcuri flagged investor mood as “marked apathy,” with Morgan Stanley’s Joe Moore seeing flows rotating into **secondary and tertiary AI beneficiaries**.[partial4] Polymarket contracts price NVDA at **66% odds** to remain the world’s largest company by year‑end, with a separate market assigning **21%** odds to an AI‑bubble “burst” scenario including a **50%+ NVDA drawdown**.[partial4] Political‑flow watchers also tracked small but repeated **Congressional sales**: Representative Daniel Meuser disclosed three partial NVDA sales of **$1,001–$15,000** each between February and April 2026.[partial4]

Strategically, NVIDIA remains at the center of AI infrastructure but faces emerging constraints and geopolitical headwinds. Management emphasized that **AI memory supply** is the main bottleneck and that its **VeraRubin system will be “supply‑constrained for the foreseeable future”** amid broader supply‑chain challenges, even as committed capacity ramps.[partial3] A separate infrastructure piece, citing IREN’s CEO, underscored that a **1‑GW “AI factory” started today might not go live until 2030**, highlighting that power, substations, permits, and grid build‑out—rather than GPUs alone—are increasingly the gating factors, with the “next phase” framed as a race to secure **megawatts** and shifting incremental value toward power and data‑center‑infrastructure names.[partial1] Another Benzinga note put the broader AI‑infrastructure spend at only **12–18% complete** against an estimated **$8T capex cycle**, with bottlenecks in **HBM, liquid cooling, copper, fiber, substations, gas turbines, and power infrastructure**, and argued that **platform companies and power/infra firms** sit on the “defensive side” of that buildout.[partial2] Geopolitically, NVDA’s **China exposure has collapsed**: Jensen Huang said on April 30 that its AI‑chip share in China fell “from roughly 90% to zero,” describing giving up China as a move that has “already largely backfired.”[partial4] U.S. export controls have further constrained China‑specific products like **H20** and **RTX 5090D V2**, while domestic rival **Huawei** is projected to grow AI‑chip revenue **60% Y/Y to $12B**.[partial4] Yet NVDA remains embedded in global AI deployment, powering **52% of tracked sovereign AI projects** in CNAS data—spanning Abu Dhabi, Poland, Japan and others—and partnering with Lenovo on a **“Lenovo AI Cloud Gigafactory”** aimed at gigawatt‑scale AI server deployments.[partial3][partial4]

For **AMD**, Friday’s nearly **5%** gain came as the market embraced its positioning as a rising AI‑accelerator and CPU platform with a more aggressive forward‑growth narrative, offsetting still‑lighter profitability. Benzinga’s comparative piece describes AMD as **“emerging as a prominent player in AI GPUs and related hardware”**, complementing existing CPU and GPU lines across PCs, gaming, data centers, industrial, and automotive.[partial1] Versus a peer group, AMD runs at **P/E 155.84, P/B 11.83, P/S 20.50, ROE 2.17%**, with **EBITDA $2.4B**, **gross profit $5.42B**, and **revenue growth 37.85%**, compared with industry averages of **P/E 153.78, P/B 14.49, P/S 18.63, ROE 9.22%, EBITDA $7.0B, gross profit $6.81B, revenue growth 48.06%**; the article flags **P/E and P/S as high vs peers**, with **P/B lower** but **ROE, EBITDA, gross profit, and growth all lagging**, implying investors are paying up ahead of current fundamentals.[partial1] Like NVDA, AMD carries a low **0.06 debt‑to‑equity ratio**, highlighted as a stronger capital structure than its top peers.[partial1] Another note tied Friday’s move to CEO **Lisa Su’s Taipei comments** that AI inferencing and **agentic AI** are driving **much stronger‑than‑expected CPU demand**, pulling CPUs back to the center of the data‑center compute mix after a GPU‑first phase.[partial4] Su said CPU supply should **expand every quarter this year**, with “much larger increases” from **2027 and beyond**, and projected the **AI‑infrastructure market could exceed $1T in 3–4 years**, while the **CPU market** could grow **>35% annually over the next five years** as inference workloads scale.[partial4] AMD is investing **> $10B** across **Taiwan’s AI supply‑chain ecosystem** to secure advanced manufacturing, packaging, substrates, and rack‑scale system capacity **through at least 2029**, and has begun ramping its **“Venice” CPUs on TSMC’s 2‑nm process**, leaning on early partnership with TSMC in both advanced manufacturing and packaging.[partial4] Su also noted that **China still accounts for ~20% of AMD revenue**; AMD is “not counting on advanced China chip sales” but will work closely with Chinese customers within U.S. export‑control rules.[partial4] Supply‑chain commentary from **Amkor** reinforced that **advanced packaging is a “major bottleneck”** for AI processors and that Amkor is working with AMD (including via TSMC Arizona) to expand capacity.[partial4] On the demand side, CNAS and Benzinga place AMD alongside NVDA and INTC as core **sovereign‑AI suppliers**, and Webull’s CEO cited AMD as a central retail AI‑chip holding, even as retail participation broadens across semis and AI infrastructure.[partial3]

**Intel** participated in the rally with a **+1.26%** close, benefiting from both sovereign‑AI narratives and speculation on strategic moves. CNAS data cited by Benzinga list INTC with NVDA and AMD as recurring U.S. hardware suppliers across accelerator chips, servers, and infrastructure in government sovereign‑AI projects, supporting a view of structurally durable data‑center demand.[partial3] Macro policy risk eased at the margin after USTR Greer said there are **“no immediate tariffs coming on semiconductors”**, even as the administration remains focused on protecting U.S. chip‑production investments, implying reduced near‑term tariff shock but ongoing policy overhang.[partial3] One Friday note said Intel “traded higher on a risk‑on tape” near recent highs and referenced **chatter that Intel has joined Qualcomm in circling AI‑chip startup Tenstorrent**, which could be valued at **> $5B**, reinforcing the idea that INTC is looking to augment its AI‑chip roadmap via M&A.[partial4] In the competitive landscape, separate NVDA‑CPU coverage framed Jensen Huang’s pursuit of a **$200B CPU market** as putting NVIDIA more squarely in competition with **Intel’s Xeon server CPU**, where the article said demand has remained strong, and mentioned NVIDIA’s **Vera Rubin** platform as part of that push.[partial2]

Among other AI‑compute names, the tape favored **supply‑chain enablers, IP, and connectivity**. **ARM** gained **3.92%** as AI/semiconductor optimism and sympathy to NVDA’s results extended a powerful multi‑month run: a buzz‑tracking piece said ARM traded around **$295–$299**, near the top of its **$100.02–$298.70** 52‑week range, and was up **128.62% 1Y / 126.67% 6M / 169.01% YTD**, with strong trend scores but a **poor value ranking** on Benzinga Edge.[partial3] Another roundup reported **Arm jumped 46.09% for the week**, supported by NVDA’s print and data‑center momentum, but also highlighted ongoing **insider selling**—including Chief Architect **Richard Grisenthwaite’s ~24k‑share sale** around May 18—and an **FTC antitrust probe** into ARM’s licensing as key risk factors.[partial2][partial3] **Astera Labs (ALAB)** added **2.48%** and has been a standout AI‑connectivity winner: Benzinga’s large‑cap gainer list noted **ALAB gained 40.61% on the week**, with the move tied to AI‑connectivity momentum and multiple **Form 144 filings** for planned insider sales totaling **280,000 shares (~$60.4M)**.[partial2] Smaller cap and IP‑levered names **CEVA +2.22%**, **AMBA +5.17%**, **SIMO +3.95%**, and **AIP +2.75%** were not tied to specific Friday headlines in this packet but traded higher alongside the broader **AI‑infrastructure buildout** narrative—centered on bottlenecks in **memory, storage controllers, connectivity, power, and cooling**—and are repeatedly described as higher‑beta satellites around the core GPU/CPU vendors.[partial2][partial3][partial4]

Across the stack, Benzinga’s coverage framed the day and week as **capital rotation within AI compute** rather than a turn away from the theme. Nvidia’s stock was portrayed as consolidating after an unprecedented run and 14 straight earnings beats, with some investor enthusiasm bleeding into **“picks and shovels”** and **second‑line AI beneficiaries** in power, cooling, packaging, memory, and connectivity, even as NVDA, AMD, and INTC remain the anchor silicon names for both hyperscale and sovereign‑AI buildouts.[partial1][partial2][partial3][partial4]
