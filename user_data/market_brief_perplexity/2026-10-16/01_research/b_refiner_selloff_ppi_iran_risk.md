## IEA diesel release [Refining margins]

- The IEA governing board said on October 15 that members would accelerate the release of remaining emergency stocks, including **more than 20 million barrels of diesel “as soon as possible,”** after approximately **330 million barrels** had already been released from the March commitment.[2] (IEA, Oct. 15; page updated Oct. 16)
- The diesel volume equals **less than one day of global diesel demand** and approximately **five days of U.S. consumption**.[1] (CNBC, Oct. 15)
- The IEA did **not specify a release timetable** on October 15; Reuters reported that Spain planned to release **4.4 million barrels** from strategic reserves during October.[3] (Reuters, Oct. 15)
- Andy Lipow, president of **Lipow Oil Associates**, said the release did not solve the supply problem because refineries damaged in the Middle East and Russia could not increase production and U.S. plants were operating at capacity.[1] (CNBC, Oct. 15)
- The release was described as an acceleration of existing commitments rather than more than **20 million barrels of entirely new supply**.[4] (Aawsat, Oct. 16)
- The available reports do not provide a quantified October 15 change in U.S. gasoline or diesel crack spreads, or a named analyst’s dollar-per-barrel estimate for the effect on VLO, MPC, PSX, DINO, or CVI.[1][3] (CNBC, Oct. 15; Reuters, Oct. 15)

## Shadow-fleet sanctions discussion [Freight and crude flows]

- The Trump administration was considering allowing **“legal oil”**—crude and refined products not subject to U.S. sanctions—to move on previously sanctioned shadow-fleet tankers; Russian and Iranian oil would remain subject to sanctions.[8] (Reuters report, Oct. 15)
- Companies seeking to use the vessels would need to apply to the **U.S. Treasury Department**.[8] (Reuters report, Oct. 15)
- The stated objective was to increase Gulf crude and refined-product flows and reduce record tanker freight rates.[8] (Reuters report, Oct. 15)
- **Energy Aspects** said the measure would have a **small** freight-rate effect because most of the affected older tankers had already been trading; it could make a small number of vessels available for Strait of Hormuz shuttle runs.[8][9] (Reuters report, Oct. 15; Baird Maritime, Oct. 15 10:10 ET)
- Maritime-industry sources said EU and U.K. insurance rules would continue to restrict the vessels, limiting any broad decline in freight rates.[8][9] (Reuters report, Oct. 15; Baird Maritime, Oct. 15 10:10 ET)
- The reports do not identify a named analyst who quantified a change in U.S. refiner crude-feedstock costs or U.S. refining margins from the proposal.[8][9] (Reuters report, Oct. 15; Baird Maritime, Oct. 15 10:10 ET)
